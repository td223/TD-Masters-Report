During compression of the polyimide sleeve, it was observed that it acted as a non-linear spring with a maxium force (shown in figure \ref{fig:stiff_char}) as 2.4N at a displacement from the relaxed state of 90mm displacement. This is consitent with literature expectations for a uniaxial loading of origami cylinders \cite{chen2022origami}. Reid (2017) compared the forces applied to different origami cyclinder designs using acetate (thickness: 0.1mm) that had been perforated with a laser to create folded regions. Reid (2017) observed that the compression of Accordion cylinders was non-linear and extremely unpredictable, however the design used was not optimally designed and therefore largely incompressible.

Extension was observed as linear with the maxium force of 2.6N with 60mm displacement relative to the relaxed length. The relaxed length of the sleeve was 120mm, the compressed length was 30mm which therefore meant that extension of the sleeve began at 90mm displacement. The extension of the device was measured to 150mm as this was the full travel of the linear stage. It was observed that around 145mm, some vertices on the sleeve inverted and therefore if compressed manual reversal of these vertices was needed. In future work, the linear stage should be configured to travel in both directions to allow a full hysteresis profile to be examined.

Due to the compression and extension of the sleeve being collected on the same experimental rig, the load cell was set tared at the relaxed length (120mm length, 90mm displacement) of the sleeve meaning that compression was measured as positive and extension as negative. However, when the data was collected it was observed that the force became zero at only 20mm displacement. This behaviour was attributed to the polyimide displaying an elastic characteristic where the folds settled into a more compressed form (cite kapton folding). This settlling behaviour may change through a full hysteresis characterisation. 

Due to the time-consuming nature of the design process, a limited number of kapton sleeves were contructed. Combined with the fragility of the sleeves and susceptibility to failure at the corners, the device was not consistently air tight. This meant that the unpressurised stiffness was characterised. This is a relatively uncommon method and due to the number of parameters related to the design of the sleeve, a direct literature comparison was not available.

Deviations from a smooth curve observed in the extension of the device were attributed to the contraint method of the sleeve.  Deformation was constrained to uniaxial compression with a wooden rod inserted through the inside of the sleeve. The friction on the inside of the sleeve on the wooden rod caused moments of higher force. In the future, a new experimental rig would be designed where the securing cap attached to the distal end of the sleeve would contain longer supporting arms that could pass through the clamp at the proximal end and remove the need for an internal constraint rod.


In the future, a control platform will be developed. This would include linear actuators to control the nitinol tendons and a vaccum/pressure pump to control the locomotion. This platform would need to allow for the fact that when constrained to a length shorter than 120mm the force required for forwards locomotion has been shown here to have a non-linear relationship to the length of the device. The unpressurised characterisation shown in this experiment sets a low bound on the stiffness of the device, when pressurised the device will exert more force axially.


\subsection{Temperature Stability of the Bellows}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Images/Masters_HeatTest_Plots.pdf}
    \caption{A) The Kapton sleeve is placed into an oven for an hour and then placed onto the stiffness characterisation experimental setup. B) The initial profile of the actuator before heating.     \label{fig:heat_test}

Temperature stability of the polyimide sleeve was evaluated through repeated exposure to high temperature (200$\degree$C, 1 hour) in an oven. This was completed on the same experimental rig as the room temperature stiffness characterisation. After 3 repetitions of heating the sleeve for an hour, it was determined that there was minimal characteristic change in the performance of the actuator after exposure to heat (Shown in \ref{fig:heat_test}). The variability in the stiffness of the unheated sleeve and the actuator that had been heated for 3 hours in total was x. Literature results are similar in their findings, with some examples showing a resistance to x $\degree$C for x cycles of x hours\cite{butler2016highly}. Therefore it was not a surprise that polyimide sleeve was unaffected. Kapton, the specific polyimide used here, has a thermal decomposition point at x $\degree$C, hence the behaviour in this constuction was as expected. The lack of behavioural change implies that the assembly would be well suited to a thermally harsh environment.  In the future, the examination of heat treatment along with other degradation techniques should be examined. In the use case of space robotics, an analysis of the UV resistance whilst heating would confirm the suitability of the sleeve.  




\subsection{Workspace Characterisation}
The workspace of a device is the set of all reachable tip positions that the device can achieve \cite{granna2014characterizing}.The resultant workspace reachable by the tip of this actuator is shown in figure \ref{fig:heatmap}. It was observed that the reachable workspace of the device in the XY plane was approximately circular, however there is slight asymmetry in the circle, with an area of high density (associated with the relaxed actuator) at a slight positive Y shift. This plot was centred on the lowest point in the Z plane which therefore indicates a slight tip bend during the actuators relaxed state. In the X-Z view, a roughly circular workspace was observed, with the actuator achieving greater than a hemisphere, which demonstrates the ability of the actuator to extend beyond the base of the device when fully actuated. The Y-Z view shows a more triangular shape, indicating that the device doesn't have a perfectly symmetrical workspace. This behaviour was attribute to the internal friction in the tendon channels causing the device to exhibit uneven behaviour.

Primary evaluation of the actuator performance was conducted through independent actuation of each tendon and monitoring the tip location in 3D space. Rotation matrices and xyz shifts were applied to the data to rotate the plots and align the base of the actuator at the origin ((x,y,z) = (0,0,0)). This was tested through 4 different assemblies of increasing stiffness, labelled ``Silicon'' for just the silicon actuator, ``Silicon with Nitinol'' for the silicon actuator with NiTi (0.6mm) inserted through the central channel, ``Kapton'' for the silicon actuator with the Kapton sleeve and finally ``Kapton with Nitinol'' as a combination of the ``Silicon with Nitinol'' surrounded with the polyimide sleeve. These constuctions were chosen to gradually increase the stiffness of the device in an attempt to decrease the variation in the device and therefore improve the controllability. It was observed that the variation in paths was lowered through increasing the stiffness, with the polyimide sleeve constraining torsional movement.

Starting with the least stiff assembly, the ``Silicon'' actuator only, it was observed that through independent actuation of the device was difficult to control, this is presented in the plots in figure \ref{fig:3d_traj}. After increasing the stiffness through the insertion of the nitinol wire in ``Silicon with Nitinol'', a more manouverable device was observed. The polar insert plots \ref{fig:3d_traj} showed that the trajectories were 120$\degree$ apart, however also observed was a twisting motion at the tip of the device, giving the plots a ``hooked'' shape. This deformation explained the reduction in vertical displacement of the actuator (see 3D plot \ref{fig:3d_traj}). When adding the polyimide sleeve to constrain the torsional deformation of the actuator in the ``Kapton'' experiment, the ``hooked'' shape was minimised, however the device did not achieve trajectories with even 120$\degree$ paths, instead the path exhibited a region of approxmately 170$\degree$ with consistent path. There was a small region of tracking observed within this region, however this was not linked to the other tracking points due to the actuator body partially occluding the reflective ball from view of the cameras. Again the polar insert plots \ref{fig:3d_traj} revealed that the independent actuation of the ``Kapton with Nitinol'' also contained a wide region with no tip tracking data. This implied that the Kapton sleeve was providing unwanted asymmetry which was attributed to the glued seam in the polyimide sleeve.
