During compression of the polyimide sleeve, it was observed that it acted as a non-linear spring with a maxium force (shown in figure \ref{fig:stiff_char}) as 2.4N at a displacement from the relaxed state of 90mm displacement. This is consitent with literature expectations for a uniaxial loading of origami cylinders \cite{chen2022origami}. Reid (2017) compared the forces applied to different origami cyclinder designs using acetate (thickness: 0.1mm) that had been perforated with a laser to create folded regions. Reid (2017) also observed that the compression of Accordion cylinders was non-linear and extremely unpredictable, however the design used was not optimally designed and therefore largely incompressible \cite{reid2017geometry}.

Extension was observed as linear with the maxium force of 2.6N with 60mm displacement relative to the relaxed length. The relaxed length of the sleeve was 120mm, the compressed length was 30mm which therefore meant that extension of the sleeve began at 90mm displacement. The extension of the device was measured to 150mm as this was the full travel of the linear stage. It was observed that around 145mm, some vertices on the sleeve inverted and therefore, if compressed, manual reversal of these vertices was needed. In future work, the linear stage should be configured to travel in both directions to allow a full hysteresis profile to be examined.

Due to the compression and extension of the sleeve being collected on the same experimental rig, the load cell was tared at the relaxed length (120mm length, 90mm displacement) of the sleeve meaning that compression was measured as positive and extension as negative. However, when the data was collected it was observed that the force became zero at only 20mm displacement. This behaviour was attributed to the polyimide displaying an elastic characteristic where the folds settled into a more compressed form \cite{dharmadasa2020characterizing}. This settlling behaviour may change through a full hysteresis characterisation. 

Due to the time-consuming nature of the design process, a limited number of polyimide sleeves were contructed. Combined with the fragility of the sleeves and susceptibility to failure at the corners, the device was not consistently air tight. This meant that the unpressurised stiffness was characterised. This is a relatively uncommon method and due to the number of parameters related to the design of the sleeve, a direct literature comparison was not available.

Deviations from a smooth curve observed in the extension of the device were attributed to the contraint method of the sleeve.  Deformation was constrained to uniaxial compression with a wooden rod inserted through the inside of the sleeve. The friction on the inside of the sleeve on the wooden rod caused moments of higher force. In the future, a new experimental rig would be designed where the securing cap attached to the distal end of the sleeve would contain longer supporting arms that could pass through the clamp at the proximal end and remove the need for an internal constraint rod.


In the future, a control platform will be developed. This would include linear actuators to control the nitinol tendons and a vaccum/pressure pump to control the locomotion. This platform would need to allow for the fact that when constrained to a length shorter than 120mm the force required for forwards locomotion has been shown here to have a non-linear relationship to the length of the device. The unpressurised characterisation shown in this experiment sets a low bound on the stiffness of the device, when pressurised the device will exert more force axially.


\subsubsection{Temperature Stability of the Bellows}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Images/heattests.pdf}
    \caption{ A) The initial profile of the actuator before heating. B) The profile after 3 cycles in the oven. C) A plot comparing the average force vs. displacement across all cycles}
    \label{fig:heat-test}
\end{figure}

Temperature stability of the polyimide sleeve was evaluated through repeated exposure to high temperature (200$\degree$C, 1 hour) in an oven. This was completed on the same experimental rig as the room temperature stiffness characterisation. After 3 repetitions of heating the sleeve for an hour, it was determined that there was minimal characteristic change in the performance of the actuator after exposure to heat (Shown in \ref{fig:heat-test}). The mamum range in the stiffness of the unheated sleeve and the actuator that had been heated for 3 hours was 0.09N and the max range was 0.44N. Literature results confirm the stability of polyimide to temperature, with some examples showing a total resistance to 275$\degree$C for over a year \cite{sroog1965aromatic} and 30,000 cycles between dry ice (-79 $\degree$) and room temperature\cite{butler2016highly}. Therefore it was not surprising that polyimide sleeve was unaffected. Kapton, the specific polyimide used here, has a thermal decomposition point at 800 $\degree$C  \cite{sroog1965aromatic}, hence the behaviour in this constuction was as expected. The lack of behavioural change implies that the assembly would be well suited to a thermally harsh environment.  In the future, the examination of heat treatment along with other degradation techniques should be examined. In the use case of space robotics, an analysis of the UV resistance whilst heating would confirm the suitability of the sleeve.  




\subsection{Workspace Characterisation}
The workspace of a device is the set of all reachable tip positions that the device can achieve \cite{granna2014characterizing}.The resultant workspace reachable by the tip of this actuator is shown in figure \ref{fig:heatmap}. It was observed that the reachable workspace of the device in the XY plane was approximately circular, however there is slight asymmetry in the circle, with an area of high density (associated with the relaxed actuator) at a slight positive Y shift. This plot was centred on the lowest point in the Z plane which therefore indicates a slight tip bend during the actuators relaxed state. In the X-Z view, a roughly circular workspace was observed, with the actuator achieving greater than a hemisphere, which demonstrates the ability of the actuator to extend beyond the base of the device when fully actuated. The Y-Z view shows a more triangular shape, indicating that the device doesn't have a perfectly symmetrical workspace. This behaviour was attribute to the internal friction in the tendon channels causing the device to exhibit uneven behaviour.

\subsubsection{Uniaxial Trajectory Characterisation}

Primary evaluation of the actuator performance was conducted through independent actuation of each tendon and monitoring the tip location in 3D space. Rotation matrices and XYZ shifts were applied to the data to rotate the plots and align the base of the actuator at the origin ($(x, y, z) = (0, 0, 0)$). This was tested through 4 different assemblies of increasing stiffness, labelled \textit{Silicon} for just the silicon actuator, \textit{Silicon with Nitinol} for the silicon actuator with NiTi (0.6mm) inserted through the central channel, \textit{Kapton} for the silicon actuator with the Kapton sleeve and finally \textit{Kapton with Nitinol} as a combination of the \textit{Silicon with Nitinol} surrounded with the polyimide sleeve. These constuctions were chosen to gradually increase the stiffness of the device in an attempt to decrease the variation in the device and therefore improve the controllability. It was observed that the variation in paths was lowered through increasing the stiffness, with the polyimide sleeve constraining torsional movement. The occurence of dexterity improving with stiffness is a known phenomenon in the soft robotics community with efforts to created devices with variable stiffness to allow for ease of manouverability and high controllabilty for complex tasks \cite{wang2022design,zhao2019soft}.

Starting with the least stiff assembly, the \textit{Silicon} actuator only, it was observed that through independent actuation of the device was difficult to control, this is presented in the plots in figure \ref{fig:3d_traj}. After increasing the stiffness through the insertion of the nitinol wire in \textit{Silicon with Nitinol}, a more manouverable device was observed. The polar insert plots \ref{fig:3d_traj} showed that the trajectories were 120$\degree$ apart, however also observed was a twisting motion at the tip of the device, giving the plots a ``hooked'' shape. This deformation explained the reduction in vertical displacement of the actuator (see 3D plot \ref{fig:3d_traj}). When adding the polyimide sleeve to constrain the torsional deformation of the actuator in the \textit{Kapton} experiment, the ``hooked'' shape was minimised, however the device did not achieve trajectories with even 120$\degree$ paths, instead the path exhibited a region of approxmately 170$\degree$ with no consistent path. There was a small region of tracking observed within this region, however this was not linked to the other tracking points due to the actuator body partially occluding the reflective ball from view of the cameras. Again the polar insert plots \ref{fig:3d_traj} revealed that the independent actuation of the \textit{Kapton with Nitinol} also contained a wide region with no tip tracking data. This implied that the Kapton sleeve was providing unwanted asymmetry which was attributed to the glued seam in the polyimide sleeve.  


\subsubsection{Multiaxial Trajectory Characterisation}


Another characterisation method investigated a multiaxial trajectory, whereby the first handle was pulled fully, then another pulled fully before slowly releasing the original handle. This method was repeated with all three handles until the actuator had returned to the starting point. This method was inspired by the out-of-phase sinosoidal actuation by Chandler (2020)\cite{chandler2020parallel}. After examining the least stiff construction, \textit{Silicon}, it was observed that there was minimal vertical movement and a polar plot indicative of tip-twisting as observed in the previous experiment. After adding the internal nitinol, \textit{Silicon with Nitinol} was observed to be more controllable. It was observed that the XY plot was more circular than without the nitinol and had a radius of approximately 50mm. Adding the polyimide sleeve to give the \textit{Kapton} construction resulted in an off-center oval-shaped XY plot that had a semi-major axis of approximately 100 mm and a semi-minor axis of 75mm. Finally, the stiffest construction, \textit{Kapton with Nitinol}, was a more regular shape in the XY plot and the greatest vertical displacement of the four contsructions. The XY plot shape had a radius of approximately 100mm. As with the \textit{Kapton} construction, the plot was not centered which was attributed to the asymmetric behaviour due to the seam in the polyimide sleeve.

\subsubsection{Variable Length of the Actuator}
% 1)State the key finding in one sentence
 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = \linewidth]{Images/Length comparison trajectories.pdf}
    \caption{A comparison of a multiaxial trajectory of a variable length continuum robot with a polyimide sleeve and a nitinol wire as a backbone.}
    \label{fig:length_comp_traj}
\end{figure}

Since this device has a variable length it is not limited in workspace to the hollow hemisphere in Fig.\ref{fig:heatmap}. This means that in comparison to other devices without this capability the workspace is a much greater proportion of the hemisphere. In medical engineering, this is an improvement on traditional surgical catheters that need to be retracted through the patient causing large shear forces on the surrounding surfaces in the body. In this polyimide-sleeved device, the retraction occurs within the body of the device to ``improve patient comfort''.

Another workspace based experiment was conducted in which the same multiaxial actuation was used to manouver the tip of the device. This was conducted at three different lengths: 60 mm, 90mm and 120mm in the \textit{Kapton with Nitinol} contruction. In the XY plane with an actuator length of 60mm, the trajectory was generally triangular with three distinct points roughly 120mm apart. As the length of the device was increased, the shape of the trajectory was observed to become more circular with a radius approximately equal to the device length. It can be observed that there was a rotation in the trajectory of the plots as the length changed. This occured as a cable gland clamped the silicon actuation device to the desired length and the tightening rotation also caused a slight rotation in the actuator. Data was also collected for the other stiffness constuctions, however the trends in the data were the same.

    \includegraphics[width = \linewidth]{Images/Stick_navigation.pdf}

To demonstrate the robots ability to navigate an arbitrary path, a route was set up using 3D printed obstacles. The goal of the path was to navigate to and deploy fluid within the area highlighted in green \ref{fig:stick_nav}.

The start position of the path required a shorter length than the relaxed length of the actuator and therefore the operator was required to apply tension to the silicon actuator in order to retract the device before steering the device using the tendons.

The effects of the bellows elastic characteristic were noticable, with the device easily extending to reach the relaxed length. After this point, overcoming the force of the sleeve pushing back on the silicon actuation device caused buckling internally due to the lack of rigidity of the silicon tube. Therefore the maximum path was limited to 120mm.


The introduction of a pressurised approach to controlling the locomotion of the device is beyond the scope of this. Adding a pressurised locomotion would stop the buckling of the device at 120mm due to the internal locomotive force applied via air-pressure rather than the silicon device. Blocked force testing of the silicon actuator would be of interest as a control method could switch between using the silicon device to push the extension of the device using air pressure.

\subsubsection{Tip-tracking and Optimisation}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = \linewidth]{Images/trajectories_with_opt_route.pdf}
     \caption{XY tip-tracking data of the arbitrary navigation past two obstacles. Time taken to complete the path is shown as the colour map. The green line shows the optimum path through 3 points correspoding to the closest path an actuator of 30mm diameter can take to the obstacles.}
    \label{fig:stick_nav_plot}
\end{figure}
An XY tip position was recorded and calculated at 0.5 second intervals, this was used to plot fig. (\ref{fig:stick_nav_plot}). This plot shows the XY position of the tip of the actuator and of the two obstacles. The colour map has been added to show the time taken in seconds to complete the path. Navigation around obstacle 1 proved challenging due to the high curvature required compared to the actuator length. At around (30, -10) this shows the region where the 3D-printed cap on the distal end of the device was colliding with obstacle 1. Across the three repeats of this navigation, this collision became less frequent through user improvement of the device control. Due to the manual control, there were moments of user error in the actuation. This was observed in the rapid movement from (90, -25) to (20, -60) and then back. An optimal trajectory was obtained with the placement of three points, these correspond to the closest points that the actuator could be to the obstacles the minimise the tip angle of the device. The line connecting these points was fitted with a polynomial of degree 3 and is indicative of the optimal tip route. The true path of the actuator featured a much greater X displacement as the extension and lateral control of the device require opposite input mechanisms. Therefore a neccessary duty cycling approach of axial growth and lateral actuation was adopted. In the future, the device will be tracked using the Optitrack cameras to allow Z-displacement to be calculated.

\subsection{Moulding of Polyamic Acid}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = \linewidth]{Images/Moulding.pdf}
    \caption{A) A conical test mould. B) Polyamic acid in NMP filling the mould. C) A failed curing of polyamic acid after heating to 150$\degree$C for 10 minutes. D) A partially successful curing of polyamic acid after drying NMP solvent for 1 week at 100$\degree$C. Sample heated to 150$\degree$C to cure. E) Resultant successful extraction of cured polyamic acid. F) Failed region at the vertex of the cone due to partial solvation of polyamic acid.}
    \label{fig:moulding}
\end{figure}
Polyamic acid undergoes a thermal imidisation reaction at 150-200$\degree$C to form polyimide. The formation of components other than films is desirable for the manufacturing of components needing the high performance properties of polyimide. An arbitrary moulded component was completed after drying PAA for 1 week of NMP.

An attempt was made by pouring PAA in NMP into a mould and heating to 150$\degree$C on a hotplate, however after 10 minutes a yellow powder was obtained. It was assumed that the lack of a unified structure was due to the solvent interrupting the curing process. Therefore a second attempt was made with the aim to remove the solvent before curing. Care was needed to avoid the thermal imidisation threshold of 150$\degree$C but whilst evaporating NMP solvent which has a higher boiling point (202$\degree$C). Therefore the use of a Smartvap was neccessary. The connecting end of the smartvap is designed for vials of 12mm diameter however it was observed that the viscosity of the polyamic acid increasing as it dried, it was decided the drying should occur whilst in the mould. Therefore an adaptor was designed in Fusion360 and printed in TPU to allow the Smartvap to connect to the top of the mould to allow the PAA to be dried in situ. After a week of drying, the TPU adaptor was removed and the mould was placed onto a hot plate set to 150$\degree$C. After 1 hour, the mould was cooled, removed and the moulded component was removed from the mould with a scalpel. The base of the cone had a smooth texture whereas at the tip, one single piece was not obtained. This was again assumed to be due to the PAA partially being in solution.
